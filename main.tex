% 1. THREE CONNECTED RESEARCH QUESTIONS
% ============================================================

\section{Three connected research questions}

AI subscription services expose increasingly heterogeneous LLMs and agents, yet
describe usage through provider-specific tokens, messages, rolling windows,
weekly pools, and feature-dependent limits. Model capability can already be
compared through common benchmarks, as illustrated by the Tencent benchmark
dashboard I observed, but the quantity of usable service remains difficult to
compare across providers. This project proposes a vendor-neutral
\emph{Standardized Usage Unit} (SUU) that complements performance information
rather than replacing it.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Q1---Economics:}
How does standardized, quality-aware usage information change the comparison
and perceived value of competing AI subscription plans?

\vspace{0.35em}

\textbf{Q2---Computation:}
How can heterogeneous provider limits and model capabilities be converted
reproducibly into comparable reasoning, coding, and agentic usage units?

\vspace{0.35em}

\textbf{Q3---Behavior:}
Does SUU improve consumers' understanding and plan selection, or can
standardization itself create anchoring and false comparability?

\vspace{0.5em}

Figure 1 connects the three questions:
computation constructs comparability,
economics interprets value,
and behavioral science tests whether standardized information improves
decision making.


% ============================================================
% 2. ECONOMICS
% ============================================================

\section{Answer to Q1: incentives and intellectual lineage}

Consider consumer $i$ choosing plan $p\in\mathcal{P}$.
Let $P_p$ denote monthly price and let

\[
\mathbf{A}_p
=
(A_{p,R},A_{p,C},A_{p,A})
\]

denote usable reasoning, coding, and agentic service.
A benchmark consumer decision is

\[
p_i^*
=
\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal P}
\left[
V_i(\mathbf{A}_p,\mathbf{Q}_p;\theta_i)
-
P_p
\right],
\]

where $\mathbf{Q}_p$ represents model capability and
$\theta_i$ captures consumer $i$'s workload needs.

\vspace{0.5em}

The consumer is the player, the action is the choice of subscription plan,
and the payoff is the perceived value of usable, quality-adjusted AI service
minus its price. The decision occurs after the provider presents price,
capability, and usage information. Consumers observe the disclosed information
but may not know how provider-specific usage units translate across competing
services.

\vspace{0.5em}

Following quality-adjusted price comparison~\cite{ehrlich2026},
my provisional hypothesis is that standardized usage information reduces
comparison costs and helps consumers select plans that better match their
workloads. However, a common unit is useful only if it preserves economically
important differences across tasks rather than hiding them.

I therefore developed an economic validation design that compares two AI
services across two workloads while varying the cost of model errors.
For each condition, the benchmark compares the plan preferred under
task-specific quality and error sensitivity with the plan that appears preferable
under a standardized aggregate measure. The central validation question is
whether SUU preserves the choice implied by economically relevant workload
differences.

This test also motivates retaining separate Reasoning, Coding, and Agentic
views rather than relying only on Overall SUU. If an aggregate measure repeatedly
changes the preferred service when workload requirements or error costs change,
the Overall measure should not be interpreted as a sufficient representation of
consumer value.

The experimental information structure changes sequentially:
Stage 1 shows original provider descriptions,
Stage 2 clarifies capabilities and limits,
and Stage 3 adds SUU.

For the current one-consumer benchmark, the decision rule is
utility-maximizing plan choice under the disclosed information.
For a later strategic disclosure extension, Bayesian equilibrium is
the solution concept under incomplete information~\cite{harsanyi1968}.
Providers could then choose how much usage information to disclose while
consumers form beliefs about service quality and usable capacity.

% 3. COMPUTATION
% ============================================================

\section{Answer to Q2: method and evaluation}

For provider $p$ and workload $j\in\{R,C,A\}$---reasoning, coding, and
agentic---let $K_j$ be the relevant benchmark set and $S_{p,k}$ the score on
benchmark $k$. I define workload quality as

\[
B_{p,j}
=
\frac{1}{|K_j|}
\sum_{k\in K_j}
\frac{S_{p,k}}{\max_q S_{q,k}},
\qquad
B_p=\frac{B_{p,R}+B_{p,C}+B_{p,A}}{3}.
\]

The full SUU framework targets a workload-specific capacity $C_{p,j}$ and
$\mathrm{SUU}_{p,j}=C_{p,j}B_{p,j}$, with separate Reasoning, Coding, and
Agentic views plus an equal-weight Overall view. Price efficiency is
$\mathrm{SUU}_{p,j}/P_p$.

The current PS1 implementation estimates only what the available data support.
Artificial Analysis v4.3 supplies common benchmark evidence and weighted-average
output tokens per Intelligence Index task~\cite{aa2026}. Let $Q_p$ be an
estimated monthly usable-token allowance, $O_p$ the reported output tokens per
standardized task, and $T_p$ the unobserved total tokens consumed by that task.
Because $T_p\ge O_p$,

\[
C_p=\frac{Q_p}{T_p}
\le
\frac{Q_p}{O_p}
\equiv C_p^{\mathrm{out}}.
\]

I therefore report $C_p^{\mathrm{out}}$ as an \emph{output-capacity upper
bound}, not as an actual monthly task count. The corresponding PS1 proxy is

\[
\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}_p=C_p^{\mathrm{out}}B_p,
\qquad
\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}_p/\$=
\frac{\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}_p}{P_p}.
\]

The benchmark mapping follows HELM's broader principle of standardized,
multi-scenario evaluation~\cite{bommasani2023}. Reasoning aggregates HLE,
GDP.pdf, CritPt, Omniscience, and LCR; Coding uses Terminal-Bench and SciCode;
and Agentic uses AA-Briefcase, GDPval-AA, and AutomationBench. The resulting
Reasoning/Coding/Agentic scores are 99.76/99.12/98.72 for ChatGPT Plus,
61.36/59.23/77.51 for Claude Pro, 75.96/66.95/84.76 for Google AI Pro, and
70.69/66.92/98.91 for SuperGrok. Their Overall quality scores are 99.20,
66.03, 75.89, and 78.84, respectively.

For capacity, the Colab uses external monthly allowance estimates of 159M
ChatGPT Plus tokens, 3.97B Claude Pro tokens, and 509M SuperGrok tokens from
Real API Pricing~\cite{realapi2026}. Google AI Pro is entered as a
low-confidence 2.22B-token monthly estimate obtained by combining two public
usage reports~\cite{googlequota2026,googleweekly2026}. Artificial Analysis
reports 27k, 118k, 71k, and 36k output tokens per task for the four selected
models. These inputs imply output-capacity upper bounds of 5,888.89,
33,644.07, 31,267.61, and 14,138.89 tasks per month. After quality adjustment,
$\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}$ is 5,841.79, 22,216.46, 23,729.62, and 11,147.41;
the corresponding upper-bound SUU-per-dollar values are 292.09, 1,110.82,
1,187.07, and 371.58.

These quantities are deliberately labeled upper-bound proxies: total task
consumption also includes input and cached-input tokens, which are not publicly
reported in the same weighted task unit. A $\pm20\%$ sensitivity check on each
quota estimate moves $C_p^{\mathrm{out}}$, $\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}$, and
SUU-per-dollar by the same $\pm20\%$, making the dependence on uncertain quota
estimates explicit. The full workload-specific $C_{p,j}$ remains a next-step
measurement problem rather than an imputed result.

\section{Answer to Q3: behavior and competing explanations}

The primary behavioral mechanism is reduced comparison complexity.
Standardized unit-price information can make heterogeneous products easier to
compare~\cite{yao2016}; SUU extends this principle to AI subscriptions.

\vspace{0.5em}

A competing explanation is \emph{numeric anchoring}.
Users may simply select the plan displaying the largest SUU even when its
workload profile does not match their actual needs.

\vspace{0.5em}

The three-stage experiment separates additional information from
standardization.
Stage 1 presents original provider information.
Stage 2 adds clearer descriptions of model capabilities and usage constraints.
Stage 3 adds the four SUU views:
Overall, Reasoning, Coding, and Agentic.

\vspace{0.5em}

Planned measures include usage-comprehension accuracy,
consistency between a user's stated workload and selected plan,
choice confidence,
and final plan choice.

Improved comprehension while preserving attention to workload and quality
differences would support the proposed mechanism.
Systematic movement toward the largest Overall SUU regardless of workload
would instead support the anchoring explanation.

The current prototype does not establish behavioral or welfare effects;
those require controlled user evaluation. In response to peer feedback, I
redesigned the behavioral validation specifically around the possibility that
Overall SUU may itself become a numeric anchor.

The revised design randomizes participants between two standardized displays:
an \textbf{Overall-only} interface and a \textbf{four-view} interface showing
Overall, Reasoning, Coding, and Agentic SUU. Participants receive a stated
workload profile and choose among the same AI subscription plans under the two
information treatments.

The primary outcome is workload-choice consistency: whether the selected plan
matches the participant's assigned workload needs. Secondary outcomes include
usage-comprehension accuracy, choice confidence, and the frequency with which
participants select the plan displaying the numerically largest Overall SUU
regardless of workload fit.

This design distinguishes useful simplification from numeric anchoring.
If the four-view interface improves workload-choice consistency while preserving
or improving comprehension, that pattern would be consistent with the claim that
disaggregation helps users interpret the standardized measure. If participants
instead converge toward the largest Overall value regardless of workload, the
result would support the anchoring explanation and motivate de-emphasizing or
removing the Overall score.

\section{Advanced development and future directions}

Herbert Simon provides a joint economics and computing foundation for this
project. His Nobel-recognized work examined decision making in economic
organizations, while Simon and Allen Newell received the A.M. Turing Award for
foundational contributions to artificial intelligence and human cognition
\cite{simonNobel,simonTuring}.

The enduring human question is how people make useful decisions when their
information and computational capacity are limited. This also provides a
continuing intellectual thread from my Week 1 proposal to the present project.

My earlier media-imitation question asked how producers interpret audience
success as a shared but uncertain signal of demand: whether observed success
reveals a persistent preference or encourages imitation of a temporary trend.
The current project applies the same broader question to AI markets. Here,
consumers interpret prices, benchmarks, usage limits, and standardized measures
as signals about the value of competing services.

The project therefore represents a reasoned pivot rather than an unrelated
change of topic. The common question is how shared information signals guide
decisions under uncertainty, and when simplifying those signals improves
coordination versus distorting behavior.

AI subscriptions create a particularly useful setting for this question because
service quantity depends on workload, model quality, error sensitivity, and
dynamic provider-specific usage rules rather than a single physical unit.

The proposed contribution integrates
computational normalization,
quality-aware economic comparison,
and behavioral evaluation while preserving workload-specific differences rather
than forcing every AI service into one opaque number.

\section{Technical details and reproducibility}

\subsection{SUU definition}

For the full target framework, provider $p$ and workload
$j\in\{R,C,A\}$ use $C_{p,j}$ to denote the number of standardized reference
tasks supported by the binding provider quota. The current PS1 does not claim
to have measured this workload-specific quantity.

Let $K_j$ denote the set of benchmark tasks associated with workload
$j\in\{R,C,A\}$, and let $S_{p,k}$ denote provider $p$'s score on benchmark
$k$.

\[
B_{p,j}
=
\frac{1}{|K_j|}
\sum_{k\in K_j}
\frac{S_{p,k}}
{\max_q S_{q,k}},
\qquad
\mathrm{SUU}_{p,j}
=
C_{p,j}B_{p,j}.
\]

\vspace{0.4em}

The reported dimensions are:

\begin{itemize}

\item
\textbf{Reasoning}:
general and scientific-reasoning capability;

\item
\textbf{Coding}:
programming and software-engineering capability;

\item
\textbf{Agentic}:
multi-step tool-use and workflow capability.

\end{itemize}

Overall SUU is

\[
\mathrm{SUU}_{p,\mathrm{Overall}}
=
\frac{1}{3}
\left(
\mathrm{SUU}_{p,R}
+
\mathrm{SUU}_{p,C}
+
\mathrm{SUU}_{p,A}
\right).
\]

Price efficiency is

\[
\mathrm{SUU/\$}_{p,j}
=
\frac{\mathrm{SUU}_{p,j}}{P_p}.
\]

\vspace{0.4em}

Equal weighting is a transparent default aggregation rule rather than a
claim that every user values the three workloads equally.
The interface therefore exposes each workload-specific SUU separately.

All model-quality weights use one benchmark source:
Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3.

\subsection{Colab computation}

The final Colab uses the selected Artificial Analysis v4.3 benchmark values for
GPT-6 Astra (max), Claude Sonnet 5 (max), Gemini 3.8 Flash (high), and Grok 4.6
(high). It computes $B_{p,R}$, $B_{p,C}$, $B_{p,A}$, and their equal-weight
Overall quality score $B_p$.

For the capacity calculation, let $Q_p$ be the estimated monthly usable-token
allowance, $O_p$ Artificial Analysis output tokens per weighted Intelligence
Index task, and $T_p$ the unobserved total tokens per task. Since $T_p\ge O_p$,

\[
C_p=\frac{Q_p}{T_p}\le\frac{Q_p}{O_p}\equiv C_p^{\mathrm{out}}.
\]

The notebook therefore reports $C_p^{\mathrm{out}}$ only as an output-capacity
upper bound. ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro, and SuperGrok use the Real API Pricing
monthly estimates of 159M, 3.97B, and 509M tokens, respectively. Google AI Pro
uses a low-confidence 2.22B-token monthly estimate derived from two public
usage reports. Artificial Analysis reports 27k, 118k, 71k, and 36k output
tokens per task for the four selected models.

The resulting output-capacity upper bounds are 5,888.89, 33,644.07, 31,267.61,
and 14,138.89. Multiplying by Overall quality gives
$\mathrm{SUU}^{\mathrm{out}}$ proxies of 5,841.79, 22,216.46, 23,729.62, and
11,147.41. The corresponding upper-bound SUU-per-dollar proxies are 292.09,
1,110.82, 1,187.07, and 371.58. A $\pm20\%$ quota sensitivity check produces
the same proportional change in these capacity-based quantities.

These values are not observed monthly task counts. The notebook intentionally
does not infer total tokens per task from an unrelated token-mix assumption;
therefore the full workload-specific $C_{p,j}$ remains future measurement.


\subsection{Current interactive artifact}

The Hugging Face artifact represents a consumer choosing among
ChatGPT Plus,
Claude Pro,

\textbf{Resulting Version 2 structure.}

The revised proposal now follows three linked validation stages.
First, the computational framework combines workload-specific quality with an
observable output-token capacity upper bound and makes the remaining measurement
gap explicit. Second, the economic validation asks whether a common measure
preserves task-sensitive differences in quality and error costs. Third, the
behavioral validation asks whether standardization improves workload-appropriate
choices or instead creates numeric anchoring. Together, these revisions change SUU from primarily a normalization formula into
a measurement-and-validation framework. A successful final SUU must use a
capacity term that is operationally comparable across providers, preserve
economically relevant heterogeneity, and improve rather than distort user
decisions.
